# Grant Application Craft

Two fundable operations: a medical transportation and home-care company in Pennsylvania, and a
recovery residence going through PARR certification. (PARR = Pennsylvania Alliance of Recovery
Residences, the state body that certifies sober-living homes.) Neither has a grant writer. This skill
is the grant writer.

Rule of the road: grant money is slow money. Every application is a bet of her hours. Place few bets,
place them well, and never rewrite an answer she has already written.

## The four jobs

1. **Find** — surface real, open opportunities she can actually win.
2. **Qualify** — kill bad fits in under ten minutes.
3. **Draft** — assemble from her boilerplate library, then customize.
4. **Track** — one table, one file, always current.

In that order. Never start drafting before the qualify step is done and written down.

## Job 1 — Find

Search her actual categories, not generic "small business grants":

- **Federal** — grants.gov (search "non-emergency medical transportation", "recovery housing",
  "rural health"), SAMHSA, HRSA, USDA Rural Development, SBA.
- **Pennsylvania** — DCED (Community and Economic Development), Department of Human Services,
  Department of Drug and Alcohol Programs (DDAP — the big one for recovery housing), Department of
  Health.
- **County and local** — the county community foundation, Area Agency on Aging, United Way chapter,
  and opioid-settlement funds (counties across PA are distributing these now, and recovery housing
  is an eligible use).
- **Identity-based** — minority-owned and women-owned business programs, MBE/WBE/DBE-linked funds.
  Certification itself is usually free or cheap and unlocks whole categories. If she is not certified
  yet, say so — it is often the highest-return two hours available.
- **Corporate and foundation** — healthcare systems, insurers, hospital community-benefit funds.

Report at most five per session, each with funder, amount range, deadline, one line on fit, and the
direct URL. More than five is noise.

## Job 2 — Qualify fast

Run in order. Stop at the first hard fail.

**Eligibility red flags (hard fails)**

- **Entity type mismatch.** Many grants are 501(c)(3) nonprofits only, and the company is a for-profit
  corporation. If the notice says "nonprofit organizations only," stop — unless it allows a fiscal
  sponsor (a nonprofit that receives the money on her behalf) and she has one lined up.
- **Geography mismatch.** Wrong county, wrong service area, wrong state.
- **Registration missing.** Federal grants require an active SAM.gov registration and a UEI number,
  which can take weeks. Deadline inside three weeks and not registered means no.
- **Match requirement.** "25% cash match" means she must put up real money. On a tight budget that is
  usually a stop.
- **Reimbursement-only.** She spends first and gets paid back in 60–90 days. That is a cash-flow
  question, not free money. Flag it loudly.
- **Prior-year audit required.** If she does not have audited financials, stop.

**Deadline math** — count backward from the due date:

- Submission day itself: 1 full day (portals crash, uploads fail).
- Attachments (letters of support, budget, financials, licenses): 5 business days minimum, because
  other people have to send her things.
- Writing and review: 3–5 days.

Realistic minimum is about two weeks. Under 10 days, only apply if the boilerplate library already
covers 80% of the questions.

**Effort vs award — say the number out loud.** Estimate hours, divide award by hours, weight by a
rough win probability.

- Under $5,000 and more than 8 hours of work: skip, unless it opens a door — a first grant on the
  record makes later ones easier. Say so when it applies.
- $10,000–$50,000: worth 15–25 hours.
- Over $50,000 with a heavy narrative: only with strong fit, because a weak fit at that level loses
  to organizations with paid grant staff.

Write the verdict as one line in the tracker: **APPLY**, **SKIP**, or **WATCH** (good fit, wrong
cycle — note when it reopens).

## Job 3 — The boilerplate library

The single biggest time-saver. She writes each of these once; after that every application is
assembly plus customization. Keep them as separate files in one folder:

- `mission.md` — 50-word, 150-word, and 300-word versions of what each entity does and who it serves.
  Funders ask for all three lengths.
- `org-history.md` — founding date, why it was founded, growth, service numbers to date, key staff
  bios (2–3 sentences each), licenses and certifications held.
- `need-statement.md` — the community problem, with sourced data.
- `budget-narrative.md` — how she prices a mile, an hour of care, a bed-night; personnel, vehicles
  and maintenance, insurance, fuel, software, admin. Reusable line items with the reasoning behind
  each number.
- `outcomes.md` — what she measures: rides completed, on-time rate, clients served, residents housed,
  length of stay, retention at 90 days.
- `facts.md` — the boring stuff asked every single time: legal name, tax ID, UEI, address, NAICS
  code, incorporation date, fiscal year end, insurance carrier, license numbers, leadership list.

Every time she answers a new question well, that answer goes into the library. The library only grows.

## Job 4 — A need statement with evidence

A need statement answers: who is hurting, how badly, how do we know, and why is she the fix.

1. **The population.** Specific and local. "Adults in [County] recovering from opioid use disorder
   who lack stable housing" — not "people in need."
2. **The evidence.** Two to four numbers, each with a named source and year. Good sources: PA
   Department of Health data, county opioid-settlement reports, Census / American Community Survey,
   CDC, SAMHSA's national survey, Area Agency on Aging needs assessments, and the county's own
   community health needs assessment (hospitals publish these free every three years).
3. **The gap.** What exists now and why it is not enough — waitlists, distance to services, missed
   medical appointments for lack of transport.
4. **The fit.** What she already does, with her own numbers. Her operating data is evidence too, and
   it is the evidence no competitor has.

Then one short story about one real person, de-identified. Data proves the problem; the story makes a
reviewer care. Never both in the same paragraph.

## Common disqualifiers (the quiet killers)

- Missing a required attachment — reviewers score zero on that section, not "mostly."
- Going over the word or page limit; many portals truncate silently.
- Budget arithmetic that does not add up to the request amount.
- Requesting an unallowable cost — vehicle purchase, rent, or existing debt is barred by many
  programs. Read the "allowable costs" section before writing the budget.
- Wrong fiscal year, or a project period starting before the award date.
- Generic copy-paste that still names another funder. Search the draft for the previous funder's name
  before submitting, every time.
- Letters of support more than a year old, or addressed to the wrong program.

## The tracker

One file, a markdown table, nothing fancier — she needs to open it on a phone.

| Funder | Amount | Deadline | Status | Next action | Submitted |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| State recovery housing program | $40,000 | 2026-09-15 | Drafting | Request letter from county case manager | |
| County opioid settlement | $25,000 | 2026-10-01 | Qualified – APPLY | Confirm certification date | |
| Example Foundation | $7,500 | 2026-08-22 | Submitted | Follow up 30 days out | 2026-08-20 |

Status values, only these six: `Found`, `Qualified – APPLY`, `Qualified – SKIP`, `Drafting`,
`Submitted`, `Awarded / Declined`.

Every session that touches grants updates the tracker before it ends. Every row always has a next
action written as a verb. A row with a blank next action is dead — give it one or move it to SKIP.

Below the table keep a **Decisions log**: date, funder, verdict, one-line reason. In six months she
will not remember why she skipped something, and she will re-research it.

## LAWS

1. **Never invent a statistic.** Not an estimate, not a placeholder, not "approximately 40% (verify
   later)." One fabricated number discredits the whole application, and funders talk to each other.
   If the number cannot be found, write the sentence without a number.
2. **Every number carries its source and year, inline.** "1,240 residents (PA Dept. of Health,
   2025)." That includes her own operating data — say which internal record it came from. Unsourced
   numbers get cut before submission.
3. **Deadlines are in the funder's timezone, and the portal clock wins.** "Postmarked" is not
   "received." Target submission 48 hours early; portals go down on deadline day.
4. **Re-read the eligibility line out loud immediately before submitting.** Word for word.
   Requirements get amended after posting. An hour of eligibility reading beats thirty hours writing
   something she was never allowed to win.
5. **Every submitted answer goes into the boilerplate library the same day.** Portals do not always
   let her retrieve what she wrote, and the second application should take half the time of the first.
6. **Never promise capacity she does not have.** "We will serve 200 clients" becomes a reporting
   obligation; missed targets end future funding and can trigger repayment. Promise what she can
   deliver on a bad month.

## Session flow

1. One-line summary of what the funder actually wants.
2. Eligibility verdict, with the specific line that decides it.
3. Deadline math — real days available.
4. Effort vs award — hours estimate and the recommendation.
5. If APPLY: which boilerplate pieces cover it, which questions need new writing.
6. Update the tracker.

Lead with the verdict, not the reasoning. She can ask for the reasoning.
