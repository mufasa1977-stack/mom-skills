# Policy Document Drafting

Two kinds of people read these documents: **residents**, who need to know what is expected of them,
and **inspectors**, who need to know this is a real, consistent, safe operation. Both have to
understand every sentence.

## The twelve documents

House rules · resident agreement · resident rights · relapse and discharge policy · governance
structure · grievance procedure · insurance record · occupancy permit record · fire safety checklist
· financial policy · good neighbor policy · code of ethics.

Treat them as one binder, not twelve separate files. They quote each other constantly. A change to
one is usually a change to three.

## LAWS

1. **One facts list is the single source of truth.** Every number, name, address, and time lives in
   one facts list. Documents pull from it. Nothing gets typed twice from memory.
2. **An unfilled `{{placeholder}}` means the document is NOT ready.** Never hand back a document
   with a live placeholder without saying so loudly, at the top, in plain words: "NOT READY — 3
   blanks left."
3. **Every document carries a version number and an effective date.** No exceptions. A document with
   no date cannot be proven current.
4. **Policy describes what she ACTUALLY does — not what sounds good.** If the policy says weekly
   house meetings, weekly house meetings must actually happen. An inspector will ask a resident.
   Aspirations belong in a plan, not a policy.
5. **Anything affecting a person's housing, money, or discharge goes to a lawyer before it is used.**
   Draft it, make it clear, then flag it for legal review out loud.
6. **Never copy another facility's document as-is.** Borrowed language describes someone else's
   building, staff, and rules. Adapt every line or leave it out.
7. **A term means one thing everywhere.** If she uses "resident," she never says "client" or
   "tenant" in another document. Pick the word once, use it in all twelve.

## The placeholder workflow

Do this once, properly, instead of filling documents one at a time.

**Step 1 — Sweep.** Read all twelve. Collect every `{{placeholder}}` into one list. Group
duplicates: `{{monthly_rent}}` may appear in five documents. It is ONE fact.

**Step 2 — Build the facts list** in a single plain file, grouped so she can answer it in one
sitting:

- **Organization** — legal name, address, phone, mailing address
- **People** — owner, house manager, on-call contact, who hears grievances
- **Money** — monthly rent, deposit, what is included, due date, late policy, refund rule
- **Daily life** — curfew, quiet hours, meeting requirement, guest hours, chore system
- **Safety** — drug testing schedule, medication storage, fire drill frequency, exit count
- **Records** — insurance carrier and policy number, occupancy permit number and dates

**Step 3 — Ask only for what is missing.** Never invent a fact. If she has not set a curfew yet,
mark it `TBD` and leave the placeholder in place, flagged.

**Step 4 — Fill everything at once** from the facts list, across all twelve documents. This is the
whole point: rent, curfew, and the manager's name can never disagree between two documents if they
came from one list.

**Step 5 — Sweep again.** Search every document for `{{`. Zero hits, or the binder is not done.
Report the count.

## The readability rule

Target an 8th-grade reading level. A resident on day three of a hard week has to understand it, and
an inspector should never have to guess what a sentence means.

- Short sentences. One idea each.
- Everyday words. "Leave" not "vacate." "Use again" not "recidivate." "Rules" not "aforementioned
  provisions."
- Active voice. "The house manager decides" — not "a determination shall be made."
- Define any term the first time it appears, in the same sentence.
- Numbers as numerals: 11:00 PM, $650, 30 days.
- Short paragraphs and real headings.

Test: read a paragraph out loud. If you run out of breath or have to re-read it, rewrite it.

## The consistency audit

Run across all twelve whenever one changes.

- **Same facts** — rent, curfew, notice periods, and names match everywhere.
- **Same words** — one term per concept.
- **Same document names** — if the resident agreement points to "the House Rules," the file is
  titled "House Rules," not "Household Guidelines."
- **No orphan references** — nothing points to a policy that does not exist.
- **No contradictions** — discharge policy and resident agreement describe the same process, in the
  same order, with the same appeal rights.
- **Same header block** on every document.

When a fact changes: update the facts list first, then every document that uses it, then bump the
version and effective date on each one that changed.

## Version and effective date

Put this at the top of every document:

```
[Facility name] — [Document Title]
Version 1.0  |  Effective: January 1, 2026  |  Approved by: [name, role]
```

Bump to 1.1 for wording fixes; 2.0 for a real change in how the house runs. Keep the old copy. Never
delete a superseded version — she may need to prove what the policy said on a given date.

## What an inspector actually looks for

- Does the binder contain every required document, signed and dated?
- Is there a current insurance certificate and a valid occupancy permit, with real numbers and dates?
- Does the fire safety record show drills actually happened, with dates and initials?
- Does the resident agreement say clearly what a resident pays, what they get, and how they can be
  discharged?
- Is there a written grievance path with a named person and a time limit for a response?
- Do residents' signed copies match the current version on file?
- Does what staff and residents say out loud match what the paper says?

The last one fails more facilities than any other. Paper and practice must agree.

## Before you call it done

1. Search for `{{` — zero results.
2. Header block present: name, title, version, effective date, approved by.
3. Every fact matches the facts list.
4. One term per concept, matching the other eleven documents.
5. Read it out loud — no sentence needs a second pass.
6. Every reference to another document points to one that exists, by its exact title.
7. It describes what actually happens in the house today.
8. Anything touching housing, money, or discharge is flagged for legal review.
9. A signature and date line where a person must agree to it.
10. The old version is saved, not overwritten.

If any item fails, say which one, in plain words, before handing it back.
